android — capture and owned displays

android — capture and owned displays

android — capture, the glasses’ camera, and displays you own

github.com/go-xrkit/android

Screen capture on Android from pure Go, CGO_ENABLED=0, for an XR compositor that redraws every frame — plus a Go API for the displays an ordinary app is allowed to make for itself, and a UVC camera client that reads the glasses' camera through usbfs on an unrooted phone.

It is the Android member of a family — go-macos/screencapture, go-freedesktop/screencast, go-mswin/screencapture — deliberately shaped so one consumer drives all of them through near-identical adapters: Displays, CaptureDisplay, Stream.Frame() (Frame, bool) lending borrowed pixels with a stride carried and never assumed, and an idempotent Close.

Two processes, because JNI needs cgo

Android hands no drawable surface to a process that is not the app, and every path to one is behind JNI, which needs cgo. So an Android build is two processes: a small Java host owning the Activity and the Surface, and an ordinary CGO_ENABLED=0 GOOS=android Go binary speaking to it over a socket, with pixels through a shared buffer. That is the pattern go-widgets/android already proved with a real installable APK.

arm64 is the only CGO-free Android architecture, and CI asserts it: it checks that arm64 links CGO-free and that arm, amd64 and 386 still require cgo, so the day that changes it is noticed rather than assumed.

What Android will and will not allow

The interesting question for an XR virtual desktop is not “can I capture the screen” — it is “can I create extra desktops and put applications on them”, the way desk does on macOS. The boundary is not your own app versus other people’s, and not virtual display versus real one. It is an activity launch versus a window, on a display you made versus a display you were given:

a display you were given (external, overlay)a display you made (createVirtualDisplay)
launch an activity on it (setLaunchDisplayId)yes — ours and Settingsno — SecurityException, even for our own activity
show a Presentation on ityesyes, and the pixels arrive

So an unprivileged APK cannot manufacture desktops for other applications. Asked four ways, refused four ways: a virtual display comes back without FLAG_TRUSTED; VIRTUAL_DISPLAY_FLAG_TRUSTED wants ADD_TRUSTED_DISPLAY, whose protection level is signature and which is not in the public SDK at all; and VirtualDeviceManager needs CREATE_VIRTUAL_DEVICE, which is internal|role and not grantable even by signature.

But it can manufacture several hundred independent displays of its own content, each rendered by the real Android view system and each read back as pixels — with no permission, no consent dialog and no screen-recording chip in the status bar. That is a ring of panels carrying what Android can draw and a Go process cannot: a WebView, a decoder, a map. It is a great deal more than one mirrored phone, and it is what the Android ribbon is built on.

On the display the glasses themselves provide — public and trusted, as any real external display is — an ordinary app may place other applications with ActivityOptions.setLaunchDisplayId. The refusal is about making a display, not about using the one you were given.

The limit is the safety

There is a ceiling on how many displays may be made at once, and past it the system dies. The ceiling observed on one emulator was 304, and that number is deliberately not hard-coded anywhere — it is one emulator’s figure. The shape of the failure is the finding, and the limit refuses in both directions rather than trusting a constant.

Coverage

100% statement coverage, gated in CI, error branches included — 87 functions, 100.0% under both go1.26.4 and go1.27.1. The whole test suite runs on all six of Go’s 64-bit targets, the four non-native ones under qemu.

⚠ Both halves of that matter together. The gate is on the TOTAL with no margin, and Go 1.27 counts more statements per block than 1.26 — 381 to 399 here on one commit — so a toolchain change moves the number under it without a line of code changing. It is harmless at exactly 100%: when every statement is covered the weights cannot matter. And because the qemu lanes EXECUTE rather than cross-compile, arch loong64 (qemu) is a real witness for golang/go#81000, whose backport is still open — it passes under 1.27.1 here, where the same lane fails in go-gfx/gfx. A code-generation defect is established by LANE, never by repository.

The camera: UVC over usbfs, on a phone nobody rooted

Since v0.12.0 this package also reads the camera the glasses carry. There is no /dev/video* for it — an ordinary Android app is refused both nodes — so the stream goes through usbfs: the Java host calls openDevice, lends the file descriptor over the socket, and the Go process drives UVC on it directly with USBDEVFS_* ioctls whose numbers are computed from this package’s own mirrors of the kernel structs and asserted against usbdevice_fs.h.

Both formats are decoded, and they are not whole in the same way:

whole by
YUY2its length, w*h*2. A short frame lost packets and is dropped, never padded
MJPEGits markers. A compressor’s output is whatever the scene made it, so “shorter than expected” describes a wall, not a loss

NewFrameAssemblerFor takes the format for exactly that reason, and UVCNegotiation.Kind is where it comes from — a negotiation result should say what was agreed rather than leave a stream to guess.

a worn head turn, replayed offline197.9° of travel in 2.9 s — out to −52°, back through zero, out to +80° — 27 of 30 frames tracked by xrkit’s headflow
live, in one process on the phone748.5° of travel over 86 matched frames in 8 s, the tracker itself costing 790 µs of an 88 ms interval — 0.9 %

⚠ Those two rows were taken under different ring depths and must not be added together. The live run delivered 11.4 fps (an 88 ms interval) and dropped 101 frames for every 91 it kept; the 23.8 came afterwards, from the fix that run exposed. A live tracking run at the new depth has not been taken.

⛔ The frame rate was OURS, not the camera’s, for five releases. StreamURBs — isochronous requests in flight — was 4, covering 4 ms of bus schedule, and any consumer costing more than that left nothing submitted while it worked. Sixteen gives 10.5 → 23.8 fps and 79 → 0 dropped on the same phone. Thirty-two makes this camera stop sending altogether, which closed that axis; so in v0.17.0 the reaping became a goroutine that only reaps, assembles and resubmits, and the decode moved to the caller’s goroutine where its cost is the caller’s.

⚠ That paragraph is half of the story, and the half that reproduces is the shape of the sweep, not its height — see 23.8 fps did not reproduce below. The ring bound the rate on the evening the camera had bytes to give; on an evening when it does not, the ring is no longer what binds.

⚠ MJPEG is slower here and WHY is open. It advertises 30 fps at every size and delivered 1.97 fps at a ring of 4, 3.21 at 16, with every loss showing a start-of-image and no end-of-image. An earlier version of this page’s upstream README called that “the camera truncates its own frames”; that reading is withdrawn. A missing tail says where the bytes stopped arriving, not who stopped sending them.

⛔⛔⛔ 23.8 fps did not reproduce, thirteen days later

2026-10-31, same phone, same glasses, same commit, in the configuration that produced it — default pick, YUY2 640×480 @25fps, payload 2400, alternate 5, StreamURBs = 16. Five runs: 9.97, 10.0, 9.98, 10.0, 9.97 fps, every one with 0 dropped and 0 late.

The ring sweep, repeated the same evening, keeps its shape and loses half its height: ring 4 → 8.91 fps and 2 dropped; ring 16 → 10.0 and none. So StreamURBs = 16 keeps its justification — it removes the dropped frames in both regimes — while “23.8 fps” turns out to be a figure of that evening’s camera state, not a property of this device.

What differs is the camera’s own byte rate for an identical negotiation: 781 bytes an interval of the 2400 it reserved, and the total is exactly ten 614 400-byte frames plus 7 992 × 12 bytes of UVC header — so nothing is missing and nothing is ours. The afternoon of 18 October it must have emitted ~1 830.

⚠ It is not the light — and that took two attempts, the first of which did not count. The obvious test pointed the glasses at a lamp in a dark room: luminance 4..86 → 37..255, byte and packet totals unchanged, and this page said refuted. It was not. A lamp saturates the MAXIMUM and barely moves the mean, and auto-exposure integrates over the mean, so the two runs may have been the same scene as far as the sensor was concerned — and a min..max range cannot say. The experiment was inconclusive and looked conclusive.

The probe reports the mean now, and the refutation that counts is a run whose mean went 22.9 → 47.7 of 255 — a true doubling — with the totals still unmoved (6 239 904 / 7 992 against 6 239 808 / 7 992). YUY2 frames are a fixed size, so its byte rate is its frame rate.

⭐⭐ And MJPEG, twice the same evening, twenty minutes apart: 3.44 and 1.71 fps — a factor of two on its own, which is why no rate comparison is offered for it either. What both runs agree on needs no comparison: 0 late (the handover never shed a frame), ≈8 000 packets a second (one per microframe, so the isochronous schedule was fully claimed) and 70 bytes an interval of the 800 reserved. Our own starvation is excluded by those invariants; why the camera truncates stays unexplained.

⛔⛔⛔ Resolved: the figure is not reproducible by its own code

The question below — which condition differed between the two evenings — assumed the 23.8 was a measurement of something. On 2026-11-01 the tag that produced it was checked out and run on the same phone and the same glasses:

# v0.16.0, the exact commit of the 23.8 fps sweep
10 frames in 998ms; 7984 packets, 6239712 bytes; 0 dropped, 0 late
ANSWER 10 WHOLE FRAMES at 10 fps

Eight runs, all ten: 9.97 · 10.0 · 9.98 · 10.0 · 9.97 · 9.99 · 9.97 · 10.0 — across two versions of the code, five scene luminance means (22.9 to 114.6), the headset worn and on a desk, and a freshly enumerated camera.

the decoupling is clearedthe old coupled reader gives the same 10 fps, so v0.17.0 cost nothing
23.8 is not reproducible by the code that produced iton its own hardware
it contradicts the byte budget of the day before832 bytes an interval (2026-10-17) allows 10.5 fps; 23.8 needs ~1 830

So it was neither a camera condition nor a regression: it is a figure that cannot be relied on. What its error was is not claimed. What the ring sweep established survives by something else — the drops falling 79 → 14 → 0 are independently reproducible, and they justify StreamURBs = 16.

⚠ One lead, from the person wearing the headset: the camera’s activity LED does not light while this code streams, though the pixels are real and their mean luminance tracks the scene across a 5× range. A UVC camera’s LED is usually wired to its capture state, so the camera may be producing frames without considering itself started — which would make the 32 % fill an expected consequence rather than a mystery.

⛔ But “the handshake is wrong” is ruled out by reading. UVC treats the move away from alternate 0 after the commit as the start signal, and three independent implementations agree with this package on both decisions that could change whether the device considers itself started:

⚠ Which also means an Android UVC viewer app would not discriminate: those are libuvc-based and take this same path through usbfs, so their LED would behave as this one’s does. And the kernel’s own path cannot be driven from an unrooted phone to compare — /dev/video0 is system:camera 0660, the shell user is not in group camera, and dd on it answers Permission denied.

What is left is that this camera commits to 2400 bytes an interval and sends ~780, consistently, in every condition tested, and that its LED may simply not be wired to UVC streaming on this unit. Neither is a defect this project can fix.

⛔ That last row spent a day in the prose as an experiment still to run, after it had been run — the eight-run list above already said “the headset worn and on a desk”. An elimination has to reach the table, not only the run list: the run list records conditions as attributes of a measurement, while the table is what a later session reads to decide what is still worth doing.

Nothing beyond it is named: temperature, firmware state, the hub and the phone’s USB power budget are all unexamined — and since the figure they were meant to explain is withdrawn, there is nothing left for them to explain.

⭐ An open question with four eliminations is worth more than an open question, and the eliminations are what a later session would otherwise redo.

⛔ The frames are pictures of a room and of whoever is wearing the headset. They go to the durable directory outside every work tree that go-appdirs/outdir chooses — never into a repository, and a .gitignore entry would be the wrong fix — and the tool prints the path so a person can look and delete.

What it does not claim

The glasses are attached now, and what that changes is marked above. Until 2026-10-18 nothing here had seen XR hardware; the capture and virtual-display figures on this page are still an arm64 Android 15 (API 35) emulator’s, and only the camera and head-tracking rows above were measured on a real phone with real glasses. The transcript is the documentation in both cases: the probe that produced every number is in the APK and can be re-run.

If you want a figure measured rather than quoted, and it is not one of the rows above, send us the hardware.
